CREDIT RISK FEATURES — PLAIN ENGLISH REFERENCE
What each field means, why it matters, and how it affects your loan assessment.


FEATURE: AGE (person_age)

What it is:
Your current age in years at the time of applying for the loan.

Why it matters:
Age is a rough indicator of financial maturity. Younger borrowers often have shorter
credit histories, lower incomes, and less financial stability. Older borrowers may
have more established finances but also more obligations.

Risk by age group:
- 18 to 22 years: Higher risk. Little credit history and often entry-level income.
- 23 to 45 years: Moderate to low risk. Peak earning and credit-building years.
- 46 to 65 years: Generally low risk. Established finances and longer credit history.
- Over 65: Moderate risk. Fixed income limits capacity for new debt obligations.

Age alone does not determine your outcome. A 22-year-old with high income and good
credit history can score better than a 45-year-old with poor credit and low income.


FEATURE: ANNUAL INCOME (person_income)

What it is:
Your total gross (before-tax) income per year in US dollars.

Why it matters:
Income determines how much of your monthly earnings would go toward loan repayment.
The higher your income relative to the loan amount, the more manageable the payments.

Risk by income level:
- Below $25,000: High risk. Very limited capacity to absorb loan payments.
- $25,000 to $50,000: Moderate risk. Manageable only for smaller loan amounts.
- $50,000 to $100,000: Low to moderate risk. Most standard loans are affordable.
- Above $100,000: Low risk. Strong repayment capacity for most loan sizes.

A high income helps only if you are not already burdened by large existing debts.
Income is most meaningful when compared to the loan amount you are requesting.


FEATURE: HOME OWNERSHIP (person_home_ownership)

What it is:
Whether you own your home, are paying a mortgage, rent, or have another arrangement.

Valid values: RENT, OWN, MORTGAGE, OTHER

Why it matters:
Home ownership reflects financial stability and asset accumulation. It also affects
how much of your income is consumed by housing costs.

Risk by status:
- OWN: Lowest risk for this feature. No monthly mortgage or rent obligation.
  You have built wealth through property and have freed-up monthly income.
- MORTGAGE: Moderate risk. You are trusted with large long-term debt (positive signal),
  but your mortgage payment competes with loan repayment capacity.
- RENT: Moderate to higher risk. No housing asset, ongoing rent reduces disposable
  income, and renters statistically have higher default rates.
- OTHER: Highest uncertainty. Unclear financial situation treated conservatively.

Renters can still receive strong loan grades if their income is high, credit history
is clean, and the loan amount is modest.


FEATURE: EMPLOYMENT LENGTH (person_emp_length)

What it is:
How many years you have been employed at your current or most recent job.

Why it matters:
Employment length is a measure of income stability. A person who has worked at
the same employer for many years is likely to continue earning a stable income.
A person who just started a new job may lose that job before the loan is repaid.

Risk by employment length:
- 0 years (less than 1 year): High risk. You may be in a probation period or recently
  unemployed. Income continuity is uncertain.
- 1 to 2 years: Moderate risk. Some stability established but still early.
- 3 to 5 years: Low to moderate risk. Reliable employment track record.
- 6 to 10 years: Low risk. Strong tenure signals the employer values you.
- 10+ years: Very low risk. Long-term employment is a strong stability indicator.

Having 0 employment years does not automatically reject your application, but it
does add risk, especially when combined with other weak factors like low income
or short credit history.


FEATURE: LOAN PURPOSE (loan_intent)

What it is:
The reason you are requesting the loan.

Valid values: EDUCATION, HOMEIMPROVEMENT, MEDICAL, PERSONAL, VENTURE, DEBTCONSOLIDATION

Why it matters:
The purpose of a loan affects how likely the money will be used productively and
whether repayment is realistic.

Risk by purpose (from lowest to highest):
- HOMEIMPROVEMENT: Lowest risk. The money improves a property asset. Borrowers are
  typically homeowners with established financial stability.
- EDUCATION: Low to moderate risk. Education is an investment in future earning
  potential, though there is risk the credential does not lead to higher income.
- PERSONAL: Moderate risk. Unspecified purpose, lenders cannot assess the use.
- MEDICAL: Moderate to high risk. Medical emergencies often coincide with income
  disruption. Financial stress from illness compounds repayment difficulty.
- VENTURE: High risk. Business investments frequently fail. If the venture fails,
  the borrower loses both business income and the ability to repay the loan.
- DEBTCONSOLIDATION: Mixed risk. Can be positive (seeking better terms) or negative
  (already struggling to manage multiple debt payments). Risk depends on total debt load.


FEATURE: LOAN AMOUNT (loan_amnt)

What it is:
The total dollar amount you are requesting to borrow.

Why it matters:
The loan amount must be manageable relative to your income. A large loan for someone
with low income creates significant monthly payment pressure.

Risk by loan-to-income ratio (loan amount divided by annual income):
- Less than 10 percent of income: Very low risk. Easily affordable.
- 10 to 20 percent of income: Low risk. Comfortably within repayment capacity.
- 20 to 35 percent of income: Moderate risk. Payment is noticeable but manageable.
- 35 to 50 percent of income: High risk. Loan is a significant financial burden.
- Above 50 percent of income: Very high risk. Payments would consume a large share of earnings.

Example: A $10,000 loan is very different for a $100,000 earner (10 percent of income,
low risk) versus a $15,000 earner (67 percent of income, very high risk).

Requesting a smaller loan amount is one of the fastest ways to improve your
chances of approval.


FEATURE: INTEREST RATE (loan_int_rate)

What it is:
The annual interest rate on the loan, expressed as a percentage (for example, 14.5 percent).

Why it matters:
Interest rate reflects how the lending market has already assessed your credit risk.
Higher rates mean higher monthly payments, which directly increases the chance that
repayments become unmanageable.

Risk by interest rate:
- 5 to 8 percent: Excellent. You are considered a prime borrower with very low risk (Grade A).
- 8 to 12 percent: Good. Low to moderate risk (Grade B to C). Standard lending terms.
- 12 to 16 percent: Elevated. Sub-prime tier (Grade C to D). Monthly payments become burdensome.
- 16 to 20 percent: High risk (Grade D to E). Lender already views you as high risk.
- Above 20 percent: Very high risk (Grade E to F). Payments consume a large share of income.

A high interest rate both reflects existing risk and creates new risk by increasing
the payment burden on the borrower.


FEATURE: PRIOR DEFAULT ON FILE (cb_person_default_on_file)

What it is:
Whether a credit bureau has recorded a formal default against you in the past.
A default means a lender previously declared you unable or unwilling to repay a loan.

Valid values: Y (yes, prior default exists), N (no prior default)

Why it matters:
A prior default is one of the most damaging items on a credit profile. Research
consistently shows that past defaulters are 2 to 5 times more likely to default again.
Even if your income and employment are strong, a default flag significantly increases
the assessed probability of default.

What it means in practice:
- N (no default): Neutral to positive. Your credit file is clean on this dimension.
- Y (prior default): Major negative factor. It does not automatically disqualify you,
  but it raises your default probability significantly and can push your grade to D or E.

The default flag remains on credit bureau records for up to 7 years from the date of
first delinquency. After that period it is removed and no longer affects your score.

How to address a prior default:
- If the default is recent, rebuilding credit actively over 12 to 24 months can
  partially offset its impact by demonstrating consistent on-time payment behavior.
- Dispute any inaccurate default records with the credit bureau in writing.
- A co-signer with a clean record can help compensate for this risk factor.


FEATURE: CREDIT HISTORY LENGTH (cb_person_cred_hist_length)

What it is:
How many years you have had an active credit file, the length of your borrowing history.

Why it matters:
Lenders use your past behavior to predict your future behavior. The longer your credit
history, the more data there is to assess whether you are a reliable borrower. A very
short history means the assessment is based on uncertainty rather than evidence.

Risk by credit history length:
- Under 2 years: High uncertainty. Lenders cannot distinguish a reliable borrower
  from an unreliable one. Risk is elevated by default because of insufficient data.
- 2 to 5 years: Developing history. Some track record but still limited evidence.
- 5 to 10 years: Solid history. Meaningful patterns of behavior are visible.
- Over 10 years: Strong history. Long track record significantly lowers risk.

A short credit history does not mean you are irresponsible. It may simply mean
you are young or new to formal credit systems. This is called a thin file problem.

How to build credit history faster:
- Open a secured credit card and pay the full balance every month.
- Become an authorized user on a family member account with good standing.
- Take out a credit-builder loan from a credit union.
- Keep old accounts open because closing them shortens your average history length.


SUMMARY: HOW FEATURES COMBINE

No single feature determines your loan outcome. The model considers all features
together. Here are the most impactful combinations:

Highest risk profile (likely Grade D or E):
- Low income relative to high loan amount
- Prior default on file
- Short credit history under 2 years
- New job or unemployed with 0 employment years
- High interest rate already assigned
- Renting with no housing asset
- VENTURE or DEBTCONSOLIDATION loan purpose

Lowest risk profile (likely Grade A or B):
- High income relative to loan amount
- No prior default on file
- Long credit history of 8 or more years
- Stable long-term employment of 5 or more years
- Low interest rate
- Homeowner (OWN or MORTGAGE)
- HOMEIMPROVEMENT or EDUCATION loan purpose

The fastest improvements you can make to your profile:
1. Request a smaller loan amount to directly reduce your loan-to-income ratio.
2. Avoid taking on new debt before applying.
3. Build 12 months of on-time payment history using a secured credit card.
4. Maintain continuous employment without gaps before applying.
5. Wait for any prior default to age off the credit bureau record after 7 years.


FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS ABOUT CREDIT RISK FACTORS

Question: I am 20 years old. Will my age alone cause rejection?
Age alone will not automatically cause rejection. However, at age 20, you likely have
a short credit history — perhaps 1 to 2 years — and an entry-level income. These two
factors in combination with a young age do increase your risk profile. The good news is
that time works in your favor. Every year of on-time payments builds your history, and
income typically grows over time. Applying for a small, manageable loan and repaying it
perfectly is the best thing a young borrower can do to build toward stronger approval
outcomes in the future.

Question: I earn $80,000. Why was I still given a high-risk score?
A high income helps, but it does not override other strong risk factors. If you have a
prior default on your credit bureau file, that single item can push your grade to D or E
regardless of income. If your requested loan amount is very large — for example, $60,000
on an $80,000 income — the loan consumes 75% of a year's earnings, which is extremely
high. If you have an existing mortgage, car loan, and credit card balances that together
consume 40% or more of your monthly income, your debt-to-income ratio is already elevated
before this new loan is added. Lenders do not look at income alone; they look at income
in relation to all existing and new obligations.

Question: I rent an apartment. Is renting always seen negatively?
Renting is not inherently disqualifying. Many excellent borrowers rent. What renting
signals to a lender is the absence of a real estate asset and the ongoing cost of monthly
rent reducing your disposable income. Renters with high incomes, long credit histories,
stable employment, and no prior defaults consistently receive strong grades. Renting only
becomes a significant risk factor when it is combined with other weak indicators such as
low income, short credit history, or a large loan request.

Question: I have been employed for only 8 months. How much does this hurt?
Employment under one year is recorded as 0 in the employment length field and is treated
as high risk for income continuity. It does not mean you are unemployed — it means the
lender cannot confirm that your income will continue beyond the near term. This is
particularly important for loans with repayment periods of 2 to 5 years. If you can
hold your current position for another 4 months and reach the one-year mark, your risk
profile improves. At 2 years, the employment risk factor reduces significantly.

Question: I am taking a loan for my small business. Why is VENTURE risky?
When you take a personal loan for a business venture, you are pledging your personal
creditworthiness against a business outcome. Business success is highly uncertain —
the majority of new businesses do not survive their first five years. If the business
fails, you lose both the business income you were counting on and your ability to repay
the personal loan. There is no business collateral behind a personal loan, unlike a
dedicated small business loan that might be secured against business assets. The risk
is that the repayment source (business profit) may disappear entirely.

Question: My medical loan was for a one-time emergency. Why is it still risky?
Medical emergencies are high-risk not because of the medical event itself, but because
of the financial disruption that often accompanies it. When you are ill or injured,
you may miss work, use up savings, or face ongoing treatment costs. A borrower applying
for a medical loan is often in financial distress at the exact moment of applying. Even
if the medical situation is now resolved, the lender cannot verify that from the
application data alone. If you can document that you are fully employed, your health
event is resolved, and your finances are stable, this context helps significantly in a
manual underwriting review.

Question: My loan amount is $5,000. Why is that considered high risk?
Loan risk is never about the absolute dollar amount. It is always about the amount
relative to your income and existing debt obligations. A $5,000 loan for someone earning
$9,000 per year represents more than half of their annual income — which is a very high
burden. The same $5,000 loan for someone earning $70,000 is less than 7% of income —
trivially affordable. Always think about your loan in terms of what percentage of your
income it represents and how it fits into your monthly budget alongside existing payments.

Question: I have no credit history at all. What can I do?
Having no credit history — sometimes called a thin file — is treated as elevated risk
because lenders have no data to predict your future behavior. This is not a penalty for
being irresponsible; it is a consequence of having no track record. The most effective
way to build credit from zero is threefold. First, open a secured credit card — a card
where you deposit the credit limit as security. Use it for small purchases and pay the
full balance every month. Second, take out a credit-builder loan from a credit union,
which works by making payments that are only released to you after the loan is fully paid.
Third, if a family member has excellent credit, ask to be added as an authorized user on
their credit card — their positive history will appear on your file. After 12 to 18 months
of this consistent behavior, you will have enough history to support a meaningful
loan application.

Question: I had a default 4 years ago but have been perfect since. Does it still matter?
Yes, it still matters, but less than it did at the time of the default. A default recorded
4 years ago will remain on your credit bureau file for up to 7 years from the date of
first delinquency. During those 7 years, it is visible to lenders and negatively affects
your grade. However, the weight the model places on a 4-year-old default is lower than
on a recent one. If your past 4 years show a clean and consistent payment record, that
positive history offsets some of the damage from the old default. At the 7-year mark,
the default drops off your file entirely, removing this factor from your profile.

Question: My interest rate is 19%. Does that mean I will definitely be rejected?
An interest rate of 19% reflects that the lender who assigned it already assessed you as
a high-risk borrower. The model interprets this as confirmation of risk — the external
market agrees with a high-risk assessment. Additionally, a 19% rate means your monthly
payments are significantly higher than they would be at 10%, which reduces how much of
your income is left for other expenses. Whether this leads to a REJECT depends on the
combination of all factors together. If your income is high, your loan amount is modest,
and all other indicators are clean, the model may still output Grade D (REVIEW) rather
than Grade E (REJECT). A 19% rate alone is not an automatic REJECT.

Question: What is the single most important thing I can do to improve my profile today?
If you have not yet applied, the single most impactful immediate action is to reduce the
loan amount you intend to request. A smaller loan relative to income directly improves
your loan-to-income ratio, which is one of the most heavily weighted factors in the
assessment. Other improvements — building credit history, reducing debt, extending
employment — take months or years. Requesting a smaller, realistic loan amount is
something you control right now and can change your outcome today.

If you have already been rejected, the single most impactful long-term action is to build
a consistent on-time payment history. This requires 12 to 24 months of disciplined
behavior but reliably converts thin-file and recovering-default applicants into approvable
profiles for modest loan amounts.


ADDITIONAL CONTEXT: HOW FEATURES INTERACT WITH EACH OTHER

Age and credit history length almost always move together. A 45-year-old with only
2 years of credit history is unusual and signals a recent credit start, possibly after
immigration, bankruptcy discharge, or prior avoidance of formal credit. The model
treats this combination with more uncertainty than either factor alone.

Income and loan amount are evaluated as a ratio, not independently. A $50,000 income
with a $5,000 loan request (10% ratio) is strong. The same $50,000 income with a
$45,000 loan request (90% ratio) is very high risk. Income is not inherently good or bad
in isolation — it only has meaning relative to the obligations being considered.

Employment length and income level work together to define income confidence. High
income with long tenure is the strongest possible signal. High income with very short
tenure is treated with moderate skepticism. The lender is asking: how certain are we
that this income will still exist in 3 years?

Prior default and credit history length interact meaningfully. A default that occurred
10 years ago on a credit file that shows 15 years of total history represents a single
lapse in an otherwise long record. A default that occurred 2 years ago on a credit file
that is only 2 years long means your entire credit history is defined by that default.
These two situations carry very different risk implications even though both show
a prior default on file.

Loan purpose and home ownership status often correlate with the type of borrower.
Home improvement loans are almost exclusively taken by homeowners, which means
this combination is reliable. A borrower listing HOMEIMPROVEMENT as the purpose
but having RENT as home ownership status is inconsistent and may prompt additional
verification during underwriting.

Interest rate and loan grade are both measures of risk, but they work at different
levels. The interest rate assigned by a prior lender is an external assessment of
this borrower's risk at a specific point in time. The grade assigned by this model
is a fresh assessment of the full current profile. When these two assessments
agree — a high interest rate corresponding to a low grade — the confidence in the
risk assessment increases. When they diverge — a high interest rate but otherwise
strong profile — the model may output a moderate grade, signaling that the current
profile has improved from the time the interest rate was originally set.

PRACTICAL EXAMPLES: WHAT GOOD AND BAD PROFILES LOOK LIKE

Example Profile 1 — Strong approval candidate (Grade A):
Age: 38 years
Annual income: $95,000
Home ownership: MORTGAGE
Employment length: 9 years
Loan purpose: HOMEIMPROVEMENT
Loan amount: $8,000
Interest rate: 6.5%
Prior default on file: N
Credit history length: 14 years

Why this profile is strong:
The person is mid-career with nearly a decade at the same employer. Their income is
high relative to the loan — $8,000 is about 8% of annual income, well below the 20%
threshold for low risk. They own a home with a mortgage, showing they have managed
large long-term debt before. No prior default on file means no behavioral red flags.
Fourteen years of credit history provides abundant data for confident prediction.
The interest rate already assigned is 6.5%, confirming an external lender also views
them as prime.


Example Profile 2 — Borderline case (Grade C or D):
Age: 27 years
Annual income: $38,000
Home ownership: RENT
Employment length: 2 years
Loan purpose: PERSONAL
Loan amount: $11,000
Interest rate: 13.5%
Prior default on file: N
Credit history length: 4 years

Why this profile is borderline:
The loan amount is about 29% of annual income — in the moderate-to-high range. The
person rents, indicating no housing asset. Employment is only 2 years, providing
some but limited stability evidence. Credit history at 4 years is developing but
not strong. The interest rate of 13.5% already signals that the market views this
as a sub-prime case. No prior default is the main positive factor. This profile
sits between Grade C and D depending on the precise combination — a manual underwriter
should review and potentially approve a smaller loan amount.


Example Profile 3 — High-risk rejection candidate (Grade E):
Age: 23 years
Annual income: $21,000
Home ownership: RENT
Employment length: 0 years
Loan purpose: VENTURE
Loan amount: $15,000
Interest rate: 21%
Prior default on file: Y
Credit history length: 1 year

Why this profile triggers rejection:
Almost every factor is at maximum risk. The person is young with a very short credit
history of only 1 year. They are unemployed or newly employed (0 years). Their income
is very low and the loan request at $15,000 represents 71% of annual income. The
interest rate of 21% is already in the extreme risk tier. There is a prior default on
file. The loan purpose is VENTURE — the highest-risk category. Any one of these factors
would elevate risk. All of them together create a profile where default probability
is extremely high.


Example Profile 4 — Recovering borrower making progress (Grade D):
Age: 34 years
Annual income: $58,000
Home ownership: RENT
Employment length: 3 years
Loan purpose: DEBTCONSOLIDATION
Loan amount: $12,000
Interest rate: 16%
Prior default on file: Y
Credit history length: 7 years

Why this profile is Grade D rather than Grade E:
This person is in financial recovery. Their default is on file but their credit
history is 7 years long, meaning the default is not recent relative to the full
history. Employment of 3 years shows stabilization. Income of $58,000 means the
$12,000 loan is about 21% of income — manageable if the interest rate comes down.
The high interest rate (16%) is a concern and a co-signer or collateral could
help convert this to a conditional approval. The DEBTCONSOLIDATION intent makes
sense in context — they are trying to consolidate and simplify existing obligations.


DEEP DIVE: ANNUAL INCOME — WHAT IT REALLY TELLS LENDERS

Income does not tell the full story on its own. What matters is net free cash flow —
the portion of income that is genuinely available to service a new loan after all
other obligations are met. Two people earning $60,000 can have radically different
repayment capacities depending on their existing obligations.

Borrower A — $60,000 income:
Monthly mortgage payment: $0 (owns home outright)
Existing car loan payment: $0 (no car loan)
Credit card minimum payment: $50
Monthly rent: $0
Available for new loan: most of monthly take-home

Borrower B — $60,000 income:
Monthly mortgage payment: $1,400
Existing car loan payment: $380
Credit card minimum payment: $250
Available for new loan: substantially less

Both borrowers have the same income. Borrower A can comfortably afford a $600 monthly
loan payment. Borrower B may struggle with $200. The model uses income and loan amount
together but cannot see all existing obligations unless they are captured in the interest
rate as a proxy signal.

Income types and their stability ranking (most to least stable):
Salaried employment income: highest stability, easiest to verify.
Hourly wage with consistent full-time hours: high stability.
Commission-based income: moderate stability, variable.
Freelance or contractor income: moderate to low stability, highly variable.
Rental property income: moderate stability, dependent on occupancy.
Investment income: variable, market-dependent.
Business profit: lowest stability, most variable.

The model records only a single income number without knowing which type of income
it represents. A salary of $60,000 and freelance income averaging $60,000 per year
carry different risks, but the model treats them identically. Human underwriters
should note income type when reviewing applications.


DEEP DIVE: EMPLOYMENT LENGTH — EDGE CASES AND NUANCES

The 0-year employment edge case:
Employment length of 0 years covers a wide range of situations. A person who started
a new job 6 months ago is very different from an unemployed person who is seeking
income through other means. The model cannot distinguish these cases. A borrower
with 0 employment years should be given the opportunity to explain their situation
during the underwriting process.

Recently promoted borrowers:
A person who was promoted to a much higher-paying role recently may show 0 or 1 year
at that salary level even if they have 10 years at the same company. Their stated
income reflects the new role, but their employment length records their time in the
new position. This can create a misleading picture. Total tenure at the company is
more meaningful than tenure in the current role in this scenario.

Self-employed borrowers and employment length:
A self-employed person's employment length is typically recorded as the number of
years their business has been operating. This is reasonable but incomplete — a 1-year-
old business is genuinely uncertain. A 5-year-old profitable business with consistent
revenue is meaningfully more stable, but the lender needs business tax returns and
financial statements to verify this, not just the stated employment length field.

Voluntary career changes versus involuntary job loss:
Someone who left one stable job for a better-paying one shows 0 years at the new
employer even though they have a strong overall work history. Someone who was laid off
or fired also shows 0 years after finding new work. The model treats both identically.
Supplementary documentation — references from prior employer, official departure reason —
can help distinguish voluntary positive moves from signs of instability.


DEEP DIVE: LOAN INTENT — WHAT BORROWERS MEAN AND WHAT LENDERS HEAR

When a borrower selects PERSONAL as the loan intent, they typically mean the purpose
does not fit neatly into the available categories, or they prefer not to specify the
exact use. From a lender's perspective, PERSONAL is the least informative category.
The model defaults to moderate risk for unspecified purposes.

When a borrower selects EDUCATION, the loan may be for tuition, books, housing costs
during study, or professional certification courses. These uses vary widely in their
expected return on investment. A certification from a recognized professional body
in a high-demand field has a very different expected income impact than a general
personal development course. The model cannot distinguish these — all appear as EDUCATION.

When a borrower selects MEDICAL, the payment target matters. Medical debt to a hospital
carries different implications than medical debt for cosmetic procedures or non-urgent
care. Emergency medical debt combined with income interruption is the highest-risk
scenario. Elective medical debt without income disruption is substantially lower risk.

When a borrower selects DEBTCONSOLIDATION, the lender should verify:
How many debts are being consolidated?
What are the current interest rates on those debts?
What is the new proposed rate on the consolidation loan?
Will the monthly payment genuinely decrease?
Will the borrower commit to closing the consolidated accounts?
Without these answers, approving a consolidation loan adds debt rather than resolving it.


DEEP DIVE: INTEREST RATE AS A RISK SIGNAL

The assigned interest rate is not just an input to this model — it is itself a prediction
made by a prior lender or credit service about the borrower's risk level. When the interest
rate is high, two things are true simultaneously.

First, a prior assessor already concluded that this borrower carries elevated risk. The
market has priced in that risk. This is corroborating evidence that the model's own high-
risk assessment is supported by an independent evaluation.

Second, the high interest rate itself creates a higher payment burden, which increases the
probability of default regardless of the borrower's initial intentions. A borrower who
would reliably repay a $300 monthly payment may struggle with the $420 monthly payment that
a 20% rate on the same loan amount produces. The rate transforms a manageable obligation
into a marginal one.

This is why a high interest rate in the model both reflects existing risk and creates
additional risk. It is simultaneously a diagnostic signal and a causal factor. Underwriters
should treat any loan with an interest rate above 16% with heightened scrutiny, not merely
because of what the rate signals but because of what it does to the borrower's monthly budget.


REFERENCE: LOAN GRADE DESCRIPTIONS AND TYPICAL PROFILES

Grade A borrowers typically have:
Credit history of 10 or more years with no missed payments
Annual income of $75,000 or more
Loan amount under 15% of annual income
Employment of 6 or more years
Home ownership of OWN or MORTGAGE
No prior default on file
Interest rate of 5 to 8 percent

Grade B borrowers typically have:
Credit history of 5 to 10 years with no recent missed payments
Annual income of $50,000 to $80,000
Loan amount of 15 to 25% of annual income
Employment of 3 to 7 years
Home ownership of MORTGAGE or RENT with good income
No prior default on file
Interest rate of 8 to 11 percent

Grade C borrowers typically have:
Credit history of 3 to 6 years, possibly with old minor negatives
Annual income of $35,000 to $55,000
Loan amount of 20 to 35% of annual income
Employment of 2 to 4 years
Home ownership of RENT
No prior default, or default resolved long ago
Interest rate of 11 to 14 percent

Grade D borrowers typically have:
Credit history of 2 to 4 years or thin file
Annual income of $25,000 to $40,000
Loan amount of 30 to 45% of annual income
Employment of 1 to 3 years or recent change
Home ownership of RENT or OTHER
Possibly a prior default on file
Interest rate of 14 to 18 percent

Grade E borrowers typically have:
Credit history under 2 years or history marked by defaults
Annual income under $30,000 for the loan size requested
Loan amount above 45% of annual income
Employment of 0 to 1 year
Home ownership of RENT or OTHER
Prior default on file
Interest rate above 18 percent
