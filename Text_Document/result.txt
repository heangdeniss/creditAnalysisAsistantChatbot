CREDIT RISK MODEL — PREDICTION OUTCOMES REFERENCE
All Possible Results, Decision Reasons, and Applicant Improvement Guidance

This document explains every possible outcome the credit risk prediction model can
return, the reasons behind each decision, and — most importantly — what a rejected
applicant can do to qualify for approval in the future.

SECTION 1 — HOW THE MODEL MAKES A DECISION

The model estimates a Probability of Default (PD): the likelihood that a borrower
will fail to repay the loan. This probability is converted to a percentage (0–100%)
and then mapped to a grade and decision.

Decision Logic:
  PD ≤ 5%   → Grade A or B → Decision: APPROVE
  PD ≤ 10%  → Grade C or D → Decision: REVIEW (manual underwriting)
  PD > 10%  → Grade E      → Decision: REJECT

Three models run simultaneously and each returns its own result:
  1. Logistic Regression
  2. CatBoost
  3. Random Forest

If any model returns REJECT, the application should be escalated for review.
A REJECT from all three models is a strong signal to decline the application.

SECTION 2 — POSSIBLE OUTCOMES AND WHAT THEY MEAN

Typical borrower profile that leads to Grade A:
  - Age: 30–55 years
  - Annual income: $80,000 or more

  - Employment length: 7+ years at current employer
  - Loan intent: HOMEIMPROVEMENT or DEBTCONSOLIDATION

  - High income means monthly repayments take up a very small share of earnings
  - Long employment shows stable, reliable income with no interruption risk
  - No prior default demonstrates consistent repayment behavior over a long history
  - Low loan amount relative to income means no financial strain from this obligation
  - Long credit history provides a meaningful statistical track record of reliability
  - Low interest rate confirms the lender's own assessment of minimal risk

Recommended action for lender:
  Approve at standard or preferred terms. Consider offering a lower rate or higher
  credit limit to build customer loyalty.

What this means:
  The borrower is a good credit risk. Default probability is low and the loan poses
  minimal financial danger to the lender. Approval is recommended with standard terms.

Typical borrower profile that leads to Grade B:
  - Age: 25–55 years
  - Annual income: $50,000–$80,000
  - Home ownership: MORTGAGE or RENT (with stable rental history)
  - Employment length: 4–7 years
  - Loan intent: EDUCATION, DEBTCONSOLIDATION, or PERSONAL
  - Loan amount: 10%–20% of annual income
  - Interest rate: 7%–10%
  - No prior default on file
  - Credit history length: 5–10 years

Why this leads to APPROVE:
  - Income is sufficient to comfortably service the loan payment each month
  - Employment tenure indicates income reliability
  - No prior default shows the borrower manages credit responsibly
  - Loan amount is proportionate to income — not overextended
  - Credit history is established enough to assess risk with confidence

Recommended action for lender:
  Approve at standard market rate. No additional documentation required beyond
  standard verification of income and identity.

What this means:
  The borrower presents a moderate risk. The model is not confident enough to auto-approve
  or auto-reject. A human underwriter should review the full application, verify income,
  and assess any extenuating circumstances before making a final decision.

Typical borrower profile that leads to Grade C (REVIEW):
  - Age: 22–30 or 55+ years
  - Annual income: $35,000–$55,000
  - Home ownership: RENT
  - Employment length: 2–4 years
  - Loan intent: PERSONAL, MEDICAL, or EDUCATION
  - Loan amount: 20%–30% of annual income
  - Interest rate: 10%–13%
  - No prior default, but short credit history (3–5 years)

Why this leads to REVIEW:
  - Moderate income limits repayment cushion if unexpected expenses occur
  - Short employment history introduces some income uncertainty
  - Loan amount is large enough relative to income to create payment pressure
  - Renter status provides less financial stability signal than homeownership
  - Interest rate indicates the borrower is not in the best credit tier
  - Thin credit file reduces confidence in risk assessment

What the underwriter should check:
  - Verify that total monthly debt obligations (rent + new loan payment) stay below
    40–45% of gross monthly income
  - Request last 3 months of bank statements to confirm income deposits
  - Check for any 30 or 60-day late payments not captured in the default flag
  - For MEDICAL intent: confirm the borrower is now employed and the health event is resolved
  - Consider approving a smaller loan amount than requested to reduce payment burden

  The borrower presents meaningful default risk. Manual review is required with strong
  justification needed to approve. Approval should come with stricter conditions such
  as a lower loan amount, higher interest rate, or requirement for a co-signer.

Typical borrower profile that leads to Grade D (REVIEW):
  - Age: 20–28 or 60+ years
  - Annual income: $25,000–$40,000
  - Home ownership: RENT or OTHER
  - Employment length: 1–3 years (or recently changed jobs)
  - Loan intent: MEDICAL, PERSONAL, or VENTURE
  - Loan amount: 30%–40% of annual income
  - Interest rate: 13%–16%
  - Possibly a prior default on file (if resolved and explained)
  - Credit history length: 2–4 years

Why this leads to REVIEW:
  - High loan-to-income ratio means payments consume a large portion of income
  - Recent employment limits confidence in income continuity
  - Higher interest rate signals the broader credit market also sees elevated risk
  - Limited credit history creates uncertainty about long-term behavior
  - Prior default (if present) requires explanation and evidence of resolution

Conditions that can convert Grade D to approval:
  - Co-signer with Grade A or B credit profile
  - Collateral or secured loan structure
  - Reducing the requested loan amount by 30–50%
  - Providing 6 months of bank statements showing consistent savings
  - Demonstrating the prior default was resolved in full with documentation

What this means:
  The model predicts a high probability that this borrower will default. The financial
  risk to the lender significantly exceeds the acceptable threshold. The loan should not
  be approved under current conditions.

  This is NOT a permanent verdict — it reflects the applicant's financial profile at
  the time of application. Many Grade E applicants can become Grade B or C within
  1–3 years through deliberate financial improvement steps.

SECTION 3 — WHY APPLICATIONS ARE REJECTED (DETAILED REASONS)

What it is:
  The borrower has a recorded default (charge-off, bankruptcy, collection account) on
  their credit bureau file (cb_person_default_on_file = Y).

Why it causes rejection:
  A prior default is the single strongest predictor of a future default. Research
  consistently shows that past defaulters are 2–5 times more likely to default again
  compared to borrowers with clean records. The model applies a large positive weight
  to this flag, pushing PD well above the 10% rejection threshold.

Example: A borrower with income of $60,000, 7 years employment, MORTGAGE ownership,
and a prior default on file will likely receive Grade D or E, even though their income
and employment are excellent. The default flag overrides otherwise strong indicators.

How to address this (see Section 4 for full guidance):
  - Wait for the negative item to age (7-year reporting period under FCRA)
  - Document the circumstances of the default and evidence of resolution
  - Rebuild credit through secured credit cards and small on-time credit obligations

What it is:
  The loan carries an interest rate above 16–18%, signaling that the market has already
  assessed this borrower as high risk.

Why it causes rejection:
  Interest rate encodes the lender's prior risk assessment AND increases monthly payment
  burden. A 20% interest rate on a $10,000 loan over 3 years produces a monthly payment
  of approximately $372. A 7% rate produces $309. For a $30,000 earner, this $63
  difference can mean the difference between manageable and unmanageable.

The model interprets high interest rate as a compounding signal: the external market
says this person is risky AND the high payment increases the probability of missed payments.

How to address this:
  - Improve credit score to qualify for lower rates
  - Apply through lenders that specialize in credit rebuilding programs
  - Consider debt consolidation to reduce total interest burden first

What it is:
  The loan amount requested is too high relative to annual income
  (loan_percent_income > 40–50%).

Why it causes rejection:
  If loan payments consume too much of monthly income, any disruption — a missed
  paycheck, unexpected bill, or reduction in work hours — immediately creates payment
  difficulty. The model treats the loan_amnt and person_income features together and
  correctly identifies when the ratio is unsustainable.

Example: A $20,000 loan on a $22,000 annual income means the loan is 91% of annual
income. Monthly payments would represent a crushing fraction of monthly earnings.

How to address this:
  - Request a smaller loan amount
  - Wait until income increases before applying
  - Apply for the loan in stages — smaller amount now, remainder later

What it is:
  The borrower has been employed for fewer than 1–2 years (person_emp_length is very low),
  suggesting recent job change, unemployment gap, or entry into the workforce.

Why it causes rejection:
  Short employment length introduces income continuity risk. If the borrower loses this
  job, they may not be able to service the loan. This is especially problematic when
  combined with other risk factors like RENT status and short credit history.

How to address this:
  - Wait until 12–24 months of continuous employment can be documented
  - Provide an offer letter or HR confirmation of permanent (not temporary) employment
  - A co-signer with stable employment can compensate for this risk

What it is:
  The stated loan intent is VENTURE (business investment) or MEDICAL (crisis-driven
  spending), which carry the highest default rates among all loan intents.

Why VENTURE causes rejection:
  Personal creditworthiness is being pledged for a business activity. If the venture
  fails — which statistically happens to 20–45% of new businesses in the first 5 years —
  the borrower loses both business income and the ability to repay the personal loan.
  There is no business collateral or financial statement to support the risk.

Why MEDICAL causes rejection:
  Medical emergencies often coincide with income disruption (time off work, disability).
  The financial stress of the medical event itself — emotional burden, recovery, ongoing
  treatment costs — compounds the risk of missing loan payments. Medical borrowers are
  often in financial crisis at the time of application.

How to address this:
  For VENTURE: Apply for a dedicated small business loan through the SBA (Small Business
  Administration) instead of a personal loan. Provide a business plan and financial projections.
  For MEDICAL: Negotiate a payment plan directly with the healthcare provider before taking a
  loan. Hospitals are legally required to offer payment plans to eligible patients.

What it is:
  The borrower's cb_person_cred_hist_length is very short (under 2–3 years), meaning the
  model has insufficient data to reliably predict behavior.

Why it causes rejection:
  Credit models are fundamentally backward-looking — they predict future behavior based
  on past behavior. With no history or very little history, the model cannot distinguish
  between a reliable borrower and a risky one. The result is high uncertainty, which
  is treated conservatively as elevated risk.

How to address this:
  - Open a secured credit card and pay the full balance monthly for 12–24 months
  - Become an authorized user on a family member's account with good standing
  - Apply for a credit-builder loan from a credit union

What it is:
  The applicant does not have a single catastrophic risk indicator but has several
  moderate ones that compound into an overall high PD above 10%.

Example combination:
  - RENT (not most stable housing)
  - 1 year employment (limited tenure)
  - $35,000 income for $12,000 loan (34% ratio)
  - PERSONAL intent (unspecified purpose)
  - 12% interest rate (subprime tier)
  - 3 years credit history
  Each alone might be Grade C. Together they push to Grade E.

Why it causes rejection:
  Risk factors multiply rather than simply add. A borrower with 3 moderate concerns
  is considerably riskier than one with 1 moderate concern, because financial stress
  from multiple angles simultaneously overwhelms repayment capacity.

How to address this:
  Focus on the highest-weight factors first:
  1. Reduce the loan amount requested (easiest immediate fix)
  2. Increase income or wait for income to grow
  3. Build employment tenure (wait 6–12 months before re-applying)
  4. Build credit history (secured card, on-time payments)

SECTION 4 — WHAT TO DO AFTER A REJECTION: IMPROVEMENT ROADMAP
Review the model output's probability value and which features are most influential.
The highest-risk factors to target first are:
  Priority 1: cb_person_default_on_file = Y (prior default)
  Priority 2: loan_int_rate > 16% (subprime rate / low credit score)
  Priority 3: loan_percent_income > 40% (too much debt relative to income)
  Priority 4: person_emp_length < 2 years (insufficient employment tenure)
  Priority 5: cb_person_cred_hist_length < 3 years (thin file)

Reduce the loan amount requested:
  If the main issue is loan_amnt relative to income, simply requesting a smaller amount
  may convert a REJECT to a REVIEW or APPROVE. Ask for 60–70% of the original amount.

Correct any errors on your credit bureau file:
  Request a free credit report from AnnualCreditReport.com from all three bureaus.
  Dispute any inaccurate negative items in writing. Erroneously reported defaults can
  be removed within 30–45 days, often dramatically changing your risk profile.

Pay down revolving balances:
  Although credit card balances are not directly in this model, they affect your
  credit score, which affects the interest rate lenders offer you. Reducing balances
  below 30% of credit limits improves FICO scores quickly (within 1–2 billing cycles).

Build employment tenure:
  Remain at your current employer without interruption. Each additional month of
  employment reduces uncertainty risk. At 12 months, many lenders reclassify a
  borrower from "new hire risk" to "established employee."

Build credit history with a secured credit card:
  Deposit $500–$1,000 as collateral and use the card for small regular purchases
  (groceries, utilities). Pay the full balance each month. After 12 months, request
  an upgrade to an unsecured card. This builds on-time payment history and increases
  credit history length.

Use a credit-builder loan:
  Offered by credit unions and community banks, these loans work in reverse: you make
  payments into a savings account and receive the money at the end. They are specifically
  designed to build credit history for thin-file applicants.

Become an authorized user:
  Ask a family member or close friend with an established, positive credit account to
  add you as an authorized user. Their account history may appear on your credit report,
  instantly lengthening your apparent credit history.

Increase income:
  The single most impactful improvement for loan affordability. Options include:
  - Negotiate a raise at current employment (supported by tenure you are building)
  - Take on a part-time second income source
  - Complete an education or certification that increases earning potential
  - Transition to a higher-paying role in the same field

Save a cash reserve (emergency fund):
  3–6 months of living expenses in a liquid savings account demonstrates financial
  maturity and creates a buffer against income disruption. While lenders cannot see
  savings directly in this model, it reduces the actual likelihood of default.

Wait out the 7-year reporting period for prior defaults:
  Negative items including defaults, charge-offs, and collections fall off credit
  bureau reports after 7 years (Chapter 7 bankruptcy after 10 years). If the default
  is 5–6 years old, waiting 12–24 more months may result in a fundamentally different
  credit profile.

Refinance existing high-rate debt:
  If current debts carry rates above 15%, refinancing at a lower rate (even 10–12%)
  reduces monthly obligations and improves the applicant's overall financial picture.
  This often requires working with a nonprofit credit counseling agency.

Apply for a secured loan:
  Pledge collateral (a vehicle, savings account, or other asset) against the loan.
  Secured loans have much lower default risk for lenders, which results in better
  approval odds and lower interest rates even for higher-risk borrowers.

Apply with a co-signer:
  A co-signer with strong credit (Grade A or B profile) accepts shared responsibility
  for the loan. This substantially reduces the lender's effective risk.
  Warning: If you default, it damages your co-signer's credit as well.

Apply to a credit union:
  Credit unions are member-owned cooperatives that typically offer more flexible
  underwriting than commercial banks. They may approve Grade D applicants that a
  bank or online lender would reject, especially for existing members.

Seek nonprofit credit counseling:
  Organizations like the National Foundation for Credit Counseling (NFCC) offer free
  or low-cost guidance for borrowers in financial difficulty. They can negotiate with
  creditors on your behalf and create debt management plans.

Explore income-sharing alternatives:
  For certain loan purposes (particularly education), income-sharing agreements or
  pay-later arrangements may service the need without a traditional loan.

SECTION 7 — FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS ABOUT PREDICTION OUTCOMES

Why do three models run at the same time?
Different machine learning algorithms have different strengths and blind spots.
Logistic Regression is strong at detecting linear relationships between features
and default risk. CatBoost and Random Forest capture non-linear patterns and
complex interactions that Logistic Regression can miss. Running all three and
comparing their outputs increases prediction reliability. When all three agree
on a REJECT, the confidence in that decision is very high. When they disagree,
it signals a borderline case that deserves human review rather than automatic action.

Do all rejected applicants have bad intentions?
No. The vast majority of people who default on loans did not intend to. Default
most commonly results from unexpected life events — job loss, medical emergency,
divorce — rather than deliberate non-payment. The model predicts statistical
risk based on financial profile, not character or intent. A REJECT result means
the financial indicators are unfavorable, not that the person is dishonest.

Why is the probability of default expressed as a percentage?
The probability of default (PD) is a numerical output from the model representing
how likely a borrower with this financial profile is to fail to repay the loan.
A PD of 8% means that out of 100 borrowers with identical profiles, statistically
about 8 would default. This continuous number allows fine-grained risk assessment
rather than a simple yes or no. The 5% and 10% thresholds used in this system are
decision boundaries set by the lender based on their appetite for risk.

Can I increase the loan amount after an initial approval?
Increasing the loan amount after approval typically requires a new application or
amendment to the existing application. The new, higher amount will be re-evaluated
against your income, employment, and credit profile. An increase that pushes
loan-to-income above the acceptable threshold may convert an APPROVE to a REVIEW
or REJECT even for the same borrower.

If I was rejected for a $15,000 loan, what amount should I apply for?
A practical approach is to calculate what loan amount keeps your loan-to-income
ratio below 30% of annual income. For example, if your annual income is $35,000,
a 30% ratio means $10,500 is the guideline maximum. Starting at 20% of income
($7,000 in this case) gives a larger margin of safety and is more likely to be
approved quickly. Once that loan is successfully repaid, your credit profile
strengthens and you can apply for a larger amount in the future.

How long are model predictions valid?
A prediction reflects your financial profile at the time of application. Your
credit score, income, employment length, and debt load all change over time.
A REJECT prediction today does not mean a REJECT in 12 months if you have
taken meaningful improvement steps. The model re-evaluates from scratch each
time you apply — past rejections are not stored in the model itself, though
they may leave traces on your credit bureau file as hard inquiries.

What does it mean if one model approves and two reject?
When two out of three models reject, the application is classified as a probable
rejection. The disagreement means your profile sits near a decision boundary —
you have some positive indicators that one model weighted more heavily, but
the overall balance of evidence leans toward risk. In this scenario, a senior
underwriter should review the full application manually with particular attention
to which specific features drove the two rejections.


SECTION 8 — EXPLAINING GRADE OUTCOMES TO APPLICANTS

How to explain a Grade A result:
Your financial profile demonstrates excellent creditworthiness. Your income is
strong relative to the amount you want to borrow, your credit history shows a
consistent, long track record of reliable repayment, you have no prior defaults
on file, and your employment history confirms stable income. The model has very
high confidence you will repay this loan in full and on time. This outcome
reflects years of disciplined financial behavior.

How to explain a Grade B result:
Your financial profile is solid. Your income can comfortably cover the loan
payments, your credit history is well established, and your employment history
indicates reliable income continuity. The loan amount is proportionate to what
you earn. While not in the very top tier, you are a dependable borrower and
this loan represents a manageable obligation for someone with your financial profile.

How to explain a Grade C result:
Your financial profile is moderate. Several positive indicators are present —
you have some credit history and steady income — but there are factors that
introduce uncertainty. The loan amount is somewhat large relative to income,
or your credit history is developing but not yet long enough to provide
strong confidence, or your housing or employment situation is less stable than
ideal. A human reviewer should look at the complete picture before deciding.

How to explain a Grade D result:
Your financial profile presents meaningful risk. The combination of factors in
your application — which may include a relatively high loan-to-income ratio,
limited credit history, short employment tenure, or high interest rate — creates
concern that repayment may become difficult if your circumstances change. This
does not mean rejection is certain; compensating factors such as a co-signer,
collateral, or a reduced loan amount can change the outcome. The model is saying
the risk is elevated without additional safeguards.

How to explain a Grade E result:
Your financial profile at this time shows a high probability of default based
on the combination of risk factors present. One or more major negative indicators
are present — such as a prior default on file, a loan amount that is very large
relative to income, very short credit history, or a very high assigned interest
rate — and these outweigh any positive factors. This outcome reflects current
financial circumstances, not a permanent judgment. Many borrowers who receive
this result can significantly improve their profile within 12 to 24 months by
taking targeted steps to reduce debt, build credit history, and stabilize income.


SECTION 9 — UNDERSTANDING PROBABILITY OF DEFAULT RANGES

What a PD between 0 and 3 percent means:
This is the range of prime borrowers. The model has very high confidence that
this person will repay. These borrowers typically have 10 or more years of credit
history with no negative marks, incomes that are high relative to their loan
request, long employment tenures, and either own their home or are paying a
mortgage. Interest rates offered to this group reflect the lender's own assessment
of minimal risk — typically 5 to 8 percent annually.

What a PD between 3 and 5 percent means:
Good borrowers. The overwhelming statistical likelihood is repayment in full. Some
minor risk indicators exist — perhaps a shorter credit history, rental housing, or
a loan amount at the upper boundary of the comfortable range — but none individually
are severe. Standard approval terms are appropriate.

What a PD between 5 and 7 percent means:
Moderate risk zone. This is the Grade C range where the model cannot confidently
auto-approve or auto-reject. The borrower has a mix of positive and negative
indicators. Manual review is the appropriate response rather than an algorithmic
decision. A human underwriter can apply judgment about context that the model
cannot capture — for example, a temporary medical situation that is now resolved,
or a recent job change to a higher-paying role.

What a PD between 7 and 10 percent means:
Elevated risk. Grade D. The financial profile shows multiple stress indicators.
Approval should come with conditions — reduced loan amount, higher rate, co-signer
requirement, or collateral. Approving at full requested terms is not advisable
without additional information or safeguards.

What a PD above 10 percent means:
High risk. Grade E. The statistical probability of default exceeds the threshold
at which lending is financially sound without extreme compensation. At this level
of predicted risk, the lender would need a very high interest rate to make the
loan economically viable, which in turn increases payment burden and makes default
even more likely. This self-reinforcing dynamic is why rejection is the appropriate
response at this level rather than simply charging a higher rate.


SECTION 10 — WHAT THE MODEL DOES NOT ACCOUNT FOR

The model makes predictions based on financial variables available at the time of
application. It does not and cannot capture every relevant factor.

The model does not know about recent life changes not yet reflected in financial data.
A borrower who received a promotion three weeks ago may still show the old, lower
income in their filed tax returns. A borrower who just paid off a large debt may not
yet see the improvement in their credit score. Recent positive changes take time to
propagate into the data the model uses.

The model does not capture character or commitment. A borrower with a thin credit file
may be extremely responsible; they simply have no track record. The model treats
uncertainty conservatively, which means responsible thin-file borrowers are
statistically disadvantaged relative to how they will actually behave.

The model does not account for local economic conditions. A borrower with a job in a
declining industry, an economically depressed region, or a role at a company that is
about to lay off workers carries risks that income and employment length alone cannot
convey.

The model does not consider the specific use of funds beyond the stated loan intent.
A borrower who says PERSONAL may have a highly specific, sensible plan. A borrower
who says EDUCATION may be pursuing a qualification in a field with poor job prospects.
The stated category is a proxy, not a complete picture.

The model does not predict recovery behavior. Some borrowers who default subsequently
negotiate settlements, enter repayment plans, and ultimately repay most of the debt.
Others walk away entirely. The model predicts default occurrence, not severity or
recovery rate.

These limitations are reasons why the model output should be used as input to a
decision, not as the decision itself — particularly for Grade C and Grade D cases
where human judgment can meaningfully complement statistical prediction.

SECTION 11 — DECISION GUIDANCE BY LOAN PURPOSE

Loans for HOMEIMPROVEMENT — Decision Framework:
Approval rate for this purpose is the highest among all loan intents. The borrower is
almost always a homeowner who is investing in their own asset. The asset itself gains
value from the improvement, which partially offsets the lender's risk exposure. A Grade C
borrower applying for a home improvement loan should be given more favorable underwriting
consideration than the same Grade C borrower applying for a VENTURE loan.

Key checks for HOMEIMPROVEMENT:
Confirm the borrower is listed as OWN or MORTGAGE in home ownership field. A RENT
status combined with HOMEIMPROVEMENT intent is an inconsistency that warrants clarification.
Verify the loan amount is proportionate to realistic improvement costs. A $50,000 request
for home improvement on a property the borrower has owned for 2 years at low income is out
of proportion and should prompt scrutiny.

Loans for EDUCATION — Decision Framework:
Education loans reflect an investment in future earning capacity. The risk window is the
period between taking the loan and achieving the expected income increase. A borrower who
is currently enrolled in a relevant program with strong employment outcomes carries lower
actual risk than the financial profile alone suggests.

Key checks for EDUCATION:
What is the borrower's current income? If they are a student with minimal current income,
the loan-to-income ratio may appear very high but reflects a temporary state, not a
permanent capacity constraint. Employment length may be 0 because they are still in school.
These contextual factors should allow a manual underwriter to moderate the model's high-risk
flags when the education investment is credible.

Loans for MEDICAL — Decision Framework:
Medical loans require careful context assessment. The financial pressure at the time of
application is real but the cause — a health event — may be resolved or resolving. A
borrower who had surgery 3 months ago, is now recovered, and is back at full-time work
is fundamentally different from a borrower who is still unable to work due to chronic
illness. The model cannot distinguish these situations from the input features alone.

Key checks for MEDICAL:
Is the borrower currently employed? Employment length near 0 combined with MEDICAL intent
may indicate ongoing inability to work. Request documentation of current employment status
and health resolution. Verify total medical debt load against income before adding a new
loan obligation on top.

Loans for PERSONAL — Decision Framework:
PERSONAL is the catch-all category. The loan purpose is unspecified and the lender
cannot assess whether the funds will be used productively. For this reason, PERSONAL
loans should be evaluated entirely on the strength of the financial profile with no
benefit of the doubt extended based on purpose.

Loans for VENTURE — Decision Framework:
VENTURE loans are the highest-risk category. The model flags these heavily and correctly.
The underwriter should ask whether a small business loan through the SBA or a business
bank account with business credit history would be more appropriate. Personal loans should
rarely be approved for business purposes unless the borrower has an exceptional overall
financial profile.

Key checks for VENTURE:
Does the borrower have a business plan? Is the business already operating with verifiable
revenue? Is the income reported on the application from wages or business income? If the
income itself depends on the business succeeding, the risk is compounded.

Loans for DEBTCONSOLIDATION — Decision Framework:
Debt consolidation signals an existing debt burden. The underwriter should calculate the
borrower's total outstanding debt before and after consolidation and verify that the new
loan genuinely reduces total monthly payment obligation. If the consolidation loan rate is
not meaningfully lower than the existing debts, it provides no benefit and the approval
should not be based on the consolidation rationale.

Key checks for DEBTCONSOLIDATION:
Verify what debts will be consolidated with documentation. Confirm the borrower will
close or stop using the consolidated accounts to prevent accumulating new debt. A
consolidation loan approval should conditionally require evidence of account closure within
30 days.


SECTION 12 — DECISION GUIDANCE BY AGE PROFILE

Very young borrowers (18 to 22 years):
These applicants almost always have short credit histories and entry-level incomes.
The model will produce low grades for most young borrowers by default. However, young
borrowers should not be disproportionately penalized beyond what their actual financial
profile warrants. Age is a risk factor only insofar as it correlates with thin credit
files and low income — the actual factors that drive risk.

A 21-year-old with a full-time job, 18 months of on-time credit card payments, and a
small loan request may be a genuinely lower risk than their grade suggests. Manual
review for young applicants requesting modest amounts is justified.

Mid-career borrowers (30 to 50 years):
This group has the strongest average profiles. Credit history is established, employment
is stable, incomes are near peak. The model performs best in this age range because the
input data is richest and most predictive. Borderline cases in this group deserve the
least benefit of the doubt because the factors are well-established.

Pre-retirement borrowers (55 to 65 years):
Special consideration applies here. Income may be high currently but retirement is
approaching. A 10-year loan to a 60-year-old who plans to retire at 65 means the last
5 years of repayment will come from pension or investment income, which the application-
reported income may not reflect. Verify the trajectory of income through retirement when
evaluating long-term loans for borrowers in this age group.

Post-retirement borrowers (65 and older):
Fixed income limits total debt capacity. Social security, pension, and investment income
can be stable and reliable, but they rarely grow and are not protected against inflation
over long loan terms. Loan terms should be shorter for this group. A 3-year loan is far
more appropriate than a 7-year loan for a 70-year-old on fixed income.


SECTION 13 — HOW TO READ A MODEL OUTPUT REPORT

When the model processes an application, the output contains several components that
should be interpreted together rather than looking at the grade in isolation.

The probability of default value:
This is the most precise output. A PD of 6.2% means the model places this borrower
between Grade C and D territory. A PD of 4.8% is near the Grade B to C boundary.
Looking at the exact number rather than just the grade letter gives more information
about how borderline or clear-cut the case is. A PD of 3.1% (Grade B) is a clear
approval with low uncertainty. A PD of 4.9% is a borderline case right at the
Grade C approval threshold.

The agreement across three models:
All three models agree on APPROVE: high confidence approval. Proceed with standard terms.
All three models agree on REJECT: high confidence rejection. Do not approve without
extraordinary circumstances and senior sign-off.
Models split 2 to 1 for APPROVE: moderate confidence approval. Proceed with normal review.
Models split 2 to 1 for REJECT: elevated risk. Escalate to manual underwriting. Do not
auto-reject but require human review before proceeding.

The grade letter:
Grade A or B: automated approval is appropriate.
Grade C: automated approval is acceptable; flag for file review within 30 days.
Grade D: requires human review before approval. Conditions should apply.
Grade E: human sign-off required. Approval should be exception-based only.


SECTION 14 — COMMON PATTERNS THAT OVERRIDE STRONG POSITIVE INDICATORS

Certain risk factors are so severe that they significantly override otherwise positive
aspects of a borrower's profile. Understanding these override patterns helps underwriters
recognize when a strong income or long employment history does not compensate for a
fundamental risk factor.

Prior default overriding high income:
A borrower with $90,000 annual income and a prior default on file will still receive
Grade D or E in many cases. The default demonstrates that they previously failed to
repay a loan even though they presumably had income at the time. The model correctly
weights the behavioral signal of a prior default more heavily than the current income
level because default history is the strongest predictor of future default behavior.

Very high loan-to-income ratio overriding clean credit history:
A borrower with 15 years of perfect payment history who requests a loan equal to 80%
of their annual income is creating a new repayment burden that bears no relationship
to their historical behavior. Their history shows they managed modest debts well. This
new obligation is of a completely different magnitude. Clean history does not guarantee
the capacity to handle any loan amount.

Zero employment length overriding strong income:
A borrower who reports $120,000 in income but shows 0 years of employment — meaning
they just started a new job this year — presents a specific uncertainty. The $120,000
may be real and documented. But if they lose this job next month, repayment collapses
entirely. Lenders must consider income stability, not just income level.

Short credit history overriding favorable loan purpose:
A borrower requesting a home improvement loan (lowest risk purpose) with only 1 year
of credit history cannot be reliably assessed regardless of purpose. The model will
still assign elevated risk because the history is too thin to provide confidence,
regardless of how sensible the loan intention is.


SECTION 15 — POST-APPROVAL OBLIGATIONS AND MONITORING

What happens after a loan is approved:
Once a loan is approved and funded, the repayment obligation begins immediately.
The first payment is typically due 30 days after disbursement. All subsequent payments
follow the agreed schedule for the full loan term.

Your obligations as an approved borrower:
Make every scheduled payment on time. Even one 30-day late payment is reported to
credit bureaus and damages your credit score. Payments do not need to be on the exact
due date — most lenders provide a grace period of 10 to 15 days — but consistently
using the full grace period signals financial stress.

Notify your lender if your circumstances change:
If you lose your job, face a medical emergency, or encounter another financial
hardship during the loan term, contact your lender before you miss a payment.
Lenders have hardship programs, deferment options, and modification agreements
available to borrowers who proactively communicate. These options are almost never
available after default has already occurred.

Impact of on-time repayment on your credit profile:
Every on-time payment you make adds to your positive payment history, gradually
building your creditworthiness. A 24-month loan repaid perfectly adds 24 months
of positive payment history to your credit file. By the time the loan is paid off,
your credit score and loan grade should be meaningfully better than when you applied —
especially if you started from a thin file or recovering profile.

What to do when the loan is fully repaid:
Check your credit report 30 to 60 days after the final payment to confirm the loan
is marked as paid in full and closed. This positive closed account now contributes
to your credit history indefinitely, unlike negative items which age off after 7 years.
